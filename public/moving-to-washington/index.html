Moving Checklist

  1. Pick a region and a city

    Washington’s two halves feel like different states. The west side (Seattle, Tacoma, Olympia, Vancouver) is mild, wet and pricier; the east side (Spokane, Yakima, Kennewick, Wenatchee) is sunnier and drier, with hot summers, cold winters and lower housing costs. Start with the seven regions and open any city for population, schools and utilities.

  2. Line up housing

    Compare listings for the specific neighborhood you want, and remember that commute times around Puget Sound can vary sharply with bridges, ferries and traffic. First-time buyers can check down-payment help through the Washington State Housing Finance Commission.

  3. Find work

    Search jobs and get free help through WorkSource Washington. The state’s Employment Security Department (ESD) runs unemployment insurance and Paid Family and Medical Leave.

  4. Set up utilities

    Electricity comes from an investor-owned utility, a public utility district (PUD) or a city utility depending on your address — see the utility list below. Water, sewer and garbage are usually handled by your city.

  5. Get your license and register your vehicles

    Within 30 days of moving, get a Washington driver license from the Department of Licensing and register your vehicles. Bring your out-of-state license and proof of identity and residence.

  6. Register to vote

    Register at VoteWA. Ballots arrive by mail and can be returned by mail or at a county drop box.

  7. Enroll children in school

    Contact the school district for your address; district and school lists are on each city page and through OSPI, the state education agency.

Taxes and Cost of Living

No income tax. Washington is one of a handful of states with no personal income tax on wages. That is the biggest financial difference for most people moving here, especially from Oregon, California or Idaho.

Sales tax. The state rate is 6.5%, and local sales taxes push the combined rate to roughly 7.6% to 10.6%. Groceries and prescription drugs are exempt. Vehicle purchases also carry sales tax plus registration fees.

Other taxes. Property taxes are set locally and vary by county and levy. Washington taxes long-term capital gains above a high annual threshold and has a state estate tax. Businesses pay the business and occupation (B&O) tax on gross receipts.

Housing. Housing is the largest cost difference between communities. Prices and rents are highest in King County and the Eastside, somewhat lower in Pierce, Snohomish, Thurston and Clark counties, and generally much lower east of the Cascades. Check current listings and the WSHFC buyer programs before you commit.

Utilities

Washington has a mix of investor-owned utilities, public utility districts (PUDs) and city-owned utilities, and the provider depends on your exact address. Each city page lists the main electric utility for that area.

  • Puget Sound Energy — electricity and natural gas across much of western Washington, plus Kittitas County
  • Seattle City Light — Seattle and some neighboring cities
  • Tacoma Power — Tacoma and nearby Pierce County communities
  • Snohomish County PUD — Snohomish County and Camano Island
  • Clark Public Utilities — Clark County (Vancouver area)
  • Avista — electricity and gas in Spokane and much of Eastern Washington
  • Pacific Power — Yakima and Walla Walla areas
  • County PUDs — Chelan, Douglas, Grant, Benton, Franklin, Cowlitz, Lewis, Grays Harbor, Mason, Clallam and other counties have public utility districts
  • Cascade Natural Gas — natural gas in many smaller cities statewide
  • NW Natural — natural gas in Clark County

For internet service, compare providers for your address on the FCC National Broadband Map.

Driver License and Vehicles

Washington gives new residents 30 days to get a Washington driver license, and vehicles must be registered here within 30 days of becoming a resident. Get your license first: you need it before you can register a vehicle. Both are handled by the Department of Licensing (DOL), and vehicle registration is also offered by county auditors and vehicle licensing offices.

Drivers in the Seattle area should know about tolls on SR 520, SR 99 and the I-405 and SR 167 express lanes, which use the Good To Go! pass. In winter, mountain passes can require traction tires or chains; check WSDOT pass reports.

Moving to Washington: Frequently Asked Questions

Does Washington have a state income tax?

No. Washington does not tax wages or other personal income. It does tax long-term capital gains above a large annual threshold, and it collects sales tax, property tax and a business and occupation (B&O) tax on businesses.

What is the sales tax in Washington?

The state sales tax is 6.5%. Cities, counties and transit districts add local sales tax, so the combined rate is roughly 7.6% to 10.6% depending on where you shop. Check any address with the Department of Revenue.

How long do I have to get a Washington driver license after moving?

New residents have 30 days to get a Washington driver license, and vehicles must be registered in Washington within 30 days of becoming a resident. You need your Washington license before you can register a vehicle.

How do I register to vote in Washington?

Register online at VoteWA with a Washington driver license or ID. Washington votes by mail: every registered voter is mailed a ballot for each election.

Where do I sign up for health insurance in Washington?

Use Washington Healthplanfinder, the state marketplace, for private plans and Apple Health (Medicaid).

What are the most affordable places to live in Washington?

Housing costs are generally highest in King County and around Puget Sound and lowest east of the Cascades. Spokane, Yakima, the Tri-Cities and many smaller towns in Central and Eastern Washington cost far less than Seattle. Compare cities on our regions pages.
