About Washington

Washington is a U.S. state in the Pacific Northwest, bordered by the Canadian province of British Columbia to the north, Idaho to the east, Oregon to the south and the Pacific Ocean to the west. It is the only state named after a U.S. president, and it became the 42nd state on November 11, 1889.

The Cascade Range divides the state in two. West of the mountains, a mild, wet marine climate supports dense evergreen forests, Puget Sound’s inland sea and the rainforests of the Olympic Peninsula. East of the Cascades, the land is drier and sunnier, with orchards, vineyards, the wheat fields of the Palouse and the scablands carved by Ice Age floods. Five major volcanoes — Mount Baker, Glacier Peak, Mount Rainier, Mount St. Helens and Mount Adams — rise along the Cascade crest, and the Columbia River crosses the state before forming most of the border with Oregon.

Most Washingtonians live in the Seattle Metro area around Puget Sound. Seattle is the largest city, followed by Spokane, Tacoma, Vancouver and Bellevue; Olympia, at the southern tip of Puget Sound, is the capital.

Economy

Washington’s economy spans technology, aerospace, trade, agriculture and tourism. Microsoft and Amazon are headquartered in the Seattle area, Boeing builds commercial jets at plants in Everett and Renton, and the ports of Seattle and Tacoma handle a large share of the nation’s trade with Asia. Health care, research (including Pacific Northwest National Laboratory in Richland) and the military, with Joint Base Lewis-McChord and naval bases around Puget Sound, are also major employers.

East of the Cascades, agriculture leads: Washington grows more apples than any other state, along with most of the nation’s hops and large crops of cherries, pears, potatoes and wheat, and it is the country’s second-largest wine producer. Hydroelectric dams on the Columbia, including Grand Coulee, supply much of the state’s electricity.

Washington has no income tax on wages, although a 9.9% tax on personal income above $1 million, signed in 2026, is scheduled to start in 2028 and is being challenged in court. The state relies heavily on sales tax (6.5% statewide, plus local sales taxes) and the business and occupation (B&O) tax, and it taxes large long-term capital gains.

History

People have lived in what is now Washington for thousands of years, and today 29 federally recognized tribes are based in the state, including the Yakama, Colville, Puyallup, Tulalip, Muckleshoot, Spokane and Makah. Spanish and British navigators explored the coast in the late 1700s, and Lewis and Clark followed the Columbia River to the Pacific in 1805.

The fur trade, centered on the Hudson’s Bay Company post at Fort Vancouver, brought the first permanent non-Native settlements. Washington Territory was created in 1853 with Olympia as its capital, and statehood followed in 1889. Railroads, timber, mining and the Klondike Gold Rush fueled growth; in the 20th century, Columbia River dams, Boeing, wartime shipyards and the Hanford site, and later the technology industry, shaped the modern state.
