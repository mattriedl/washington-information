Cost of Living in Washington State: A Region-by-Region Breakdown

Housing drives the cost of living here. See what rent, taxes and everyday costs look like from Seattle to Stevens County.

📅 November 14, 2026⏱ 8 min read
Home › Blog › Cost of Living in Washington State: Rent, Taxes & Cheapest Places

The cost of living in Washington State runs about 7% above the national average, according to the federal government’s price index, and housing is what drives it. A two-bedroom rental in the Seattle area costs nearly twice as much as one in Stevens County. Washington does not tax wages, but sales, gas and payroll taxes are higher than in most states. Here is what it really costs to live here, region by region.

Quick answer

  • Overall: Washington’s price level was 107.0 in 2024 (U.S. = 100); the Seattle metro was 111.1.
  • Housing: the typical Washington home is worth about $581,000 (Zillow, October 2026), and the statewide fair market rent for a two-bedroom is $2,072 a month.
  • Income needed: a full-time worker needs about $39.84 an hour to afford that two-bedroom without spending more than 30% of income on rent.
  • Taxes: no tax on wages, but a 6.5% state sales tax (7.6% to 10.6% combined) and a 55-cent gas tax.
  • Cheapest areas: Stevens County, Yakima, Longview-Kelso and rural Eastern Washington.

Is Washington State expensive to live in?

Yes, somewhat: Washington is more expensive than most states, but cheaper than California, Hawaii or the Northeast’s big metros. The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes Regional Price Parities, which compare the price of the same basket of goods and services across states. In 2024 Washington scored 107.0, meaning prices were about 7% above the national average (BEA via FRED). The Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metro scored 111.1 (BEA via FRED).

Most of that premium is housing. Washington’s housing price level was 126.0, or 26% above average (BEA via FRED), while everyday goods cost only slightly more than elsewhere. For comparison, California’s overall index was 110.7 and its housing index 154.3.

The statewide average hides a big split. The Puget Sound region, where more than half of Washingtonians live, is expensive. East of the Cascades, in cities like Spokane, Yakima and Kennewick, housing costs are much closer to the national norm.

Housing costs: rent and home prices

The typical home in Washington is worth about $581,000, down 0.9% over the past year, according to the Zillow Home Value Index as of October 31, 2026 (Zillow). That is well above the national typical value but roughly $180,000 below California’s.

For renters, the best statewide benchmark is the fair market rent used by HUD for housing vouchers, which reflects a modest, non-luxury unit including utilities. The National Low Income Housing Coalition’s 2026 Out of Reach report puts Washington’s two-bedroom fair market rent at $2,072 a month. In the Seattle-Bellevue area it is $2,501.

A few housing costs catch newcomers off guard:

  • Commute premiums. Homes near light rail, ferries or Eastside employers carry higher prices; the same budget buys much more space in Pierce, Thurston or Snohomish County.
  • Property taxes. They are set locally by levies and vary widely by county and school district. Check a specific address with the county assessor before you buy.
  • Insurance. Earthquake coverage is a separate policy, and homes in wildfire-prone areas east of the Cascades can be harder to insure.

Taxes: what Washington charges instead of income tax

Washington does not tax wages, so it collects more through sales, property, fuel and business taxes. How that nets out depends on how much you earn and spend.

Sales tax

The state rate is 6.5%. Cities, counties and transit districts add their own, so combined rates run from 7.6% in Goldendale and rural Klickitat County to 10.6% in Edmonds, Lynnwood, Mill Creek and Mukilteo (MRSC). Groceries and prescription drugs are exempt. You can look up the rate for any address with the Department of Revenue.

Capital gains and high-income taxes

Long-term capital gains above a large annual exemption are taxed at 7%, plus 2.9% on gains over $1 million, beginning with 2025 gains (Kiplinger). A 9.9% tax on personal income above $1 million was signed in 2026 and is scheduled to start in 2028; it is being challenged in court. Neither affects typical households.

Payroll programs

Workers pay into the state’s Paid Family and Medical Leave program (a 1.13% premium in 2026, about 71% of it paid by the employee, per the Employment Security Department) and most pay 0.58% of wages into WA Cares, the long-term care program.

Utilities, gas and transportation

Electricity is one of Washington’s bargains; gasoline is not.

  • Electricity. Thanks largely to hydropower, Washington’s average retail electricity price was 10.13 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2024, among the lowest in the country (U.S. Energy Information Administration). Rates vary by utility; public utility districts in Central Washington are often cheapest.
  • Heating. Many homes heat with electricity or natural gas. Mild west-side winters keep heating bills modest, while Spokane and other east-side cities have colder winters and higher heating costs.
  • Gas. The state gas tax is 55.4 cents a gallon since July 2025, third highest in the nation, and now rises about 2% a year (Cascade PBS).
  • Car tabs and tolls. Inside the Sound Transit district, vehicle registration includes a regional transit tax, and drivers pay tolls on SR 520, SR 99 and the express lanes of I-405 and SR 167.
  • Transit. In the Seattle area, Link light rail and frequent buses let many households get by with one car or none, which can offset higher rents.

How much do you need to live comfortably in Washington State?

To afford a modest two-bedroom on one income, you need about $39.84 an hour statewide, or about $82,900 a year, and about $48.10 an hour (about $100,000 a year) in the Seattle area. Those are the 2026 Out of Reach housing wages, based on spending no more than 30% of income on rent.

Compare that with the minimum wage: Washington’s is $17.13 an hour in 2026 (Department of Labor & Industries) and Seattle’s is $21.30. Even at those rates, which are among the highest in the country, a single minimum-wage earner cannot afford a two-bedroom at fair market rent anywhere in the state, which is why roommates and two-income households are common.

A practical way to budget: start with rent or a mortgage for the specific city you are considering, add utilities (lower than average), transportation (higher than average if you drive a lot), and remember that the money you would have paid in state income tax elsewhere partly offsets Washington’s sales tax.

Cheapest places to live in Washington State

The most affordable parts of Washington are east of the Cascades and in the southwest outside the Portland suburbs. Based on fair market rents and typical home prices, these areas offer the most for your money:

Stevens County and the northeast

Stevens County has the lowest two-bedroom fair market rent in the state, $1,131. Colville and Chewelah are the main towns, about an hour or more north of Spokane, with forests, lakes and cold winters.

Yakima and the Yakima Valley

Yakima ($1,374) and nearby Selah, Union Gap and Sunnyside combine low rents with sunshine and wine country. Wages are also lower, so compare carefully if you will work locally.

Longview and Kelso

Longview and Kelso ($1,451) sit on I-5 about an hour north of Portland, making them a value option in Southwest Washington.

Spokane and the Tri-Cities

Spokane ($1,531) and Kennewick, Richland and Pasco ($1,538) offer big-city services, airports and hospitals for far less than Puget Sound.

Small towns everywhere

Rural Washington averages $1,299 for a two-bedroom. Our list of the best small towns in Washington State includes several affordable choices.

Frequently asked questions

Is Washington State expensive to live in?

Washington is more expensive than average: federal data put prices about 7% above the national level in 2024, and housing about 26% above it. The Seattle area is the most expensive part of the state; Eastern Washington is close to the national average.

What salary do you need to live comfortably in Washington?

A full-time worker needs about $39.84 an hour, roughly $82,900 a year, to afford a modest two-bedroom at the statewide fair market rent while spending no more than 30% of income on rent. In the Seattle-Bellevue area the figure is about $48.10 an hour, roughly $100,000 a year.

What is the cheapest place to live in Washington State?

Stevens County in the northeast has the lowest two-bedroom fair market rent in the state at $1,131 a month. Yakima, Longview-Kelso and rural Eastern Washington are also among the most affordable areas.

Is it cheaper to live in Washington or Oregon?

They are close. Washington has no tax on wages but a high sales tax; Oregon has no sales tax but a state income tax of up to 9.9%. Which is cheaper depends on your income, spending and the specific cities you compare.

Are utilities expensive in Washington?

No. Washington’s average electricity price was about 10 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2024, among the lowest in the country, largely because of hydropower.

Does Washington have sales tax on groceries?

No. Most groceries and prescription drugs are exempt from Washington sales tax. Prepared food, restaurant meals and most other purchases are taxed.
